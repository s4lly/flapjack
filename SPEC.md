# Flapjack — v1 Spec

## Product

A lightweight macOS desktop flip clock. The clock face is the entire app: hours and minutes as large split-flap digits.

## Requirements

### Clock display
- Split-flap ("flip clock") style hours and minutes, e.g. `10:07`, with a flip animation when a digit changes.
- 12-hour display; seconds are not shown. The AM/PM indicator is a small, subtle badge tucked into the bottom-right corner of the last minute card — always in-card, never a column beside the digits, in either layout below. Keeping it out of the layout reserves the horizontal space for future features and lets the digits scale larger. It is included in the face's accessibility label, since it is visually small by design.
- Digits use a monospaced/tabular look so the layout doesn't jitter.
- **Adaptive layout.** The four cards arrange themselves two ways: *across* (`HH:MM` on one row, colon between) and *stacked* (`HH` over `MM`). Each arrangement derives its own unit from its own content aspect, and the face draws whichever one yields the **larger** unit for the region it is given — so the choice is simply "whichever makes the digits bigger". Comparing units rather than testing the region's aspect against a hand-picked threshold is what makes it correct: the crossover (~1.47 for the current proportions) falls out of the comparison, and the degenerate bands, where both arrangements are bound by the same dimension, resolve on their own. A guessed 2.0 threshold was measurably wrong.
- The rule is pure geometry and applies at all times, not only when the events panel is open: a tall window with no panel stacks too. It re-evaluates live as the window resizes or the events divider is dragged, and the switch itself is not animated — an arrangement change is a relayout, not a transition — while the per-digit flip animation is unaffected inside either arrangement.
- When stacked, the colon's two dots are laid **horizontally** in a ~0.26-unit gutter between the rows. The two rows already read as two pairs, but the dots keep the face recognisably a clock rather than a stack of numbers, and a gutter that wide leaves them crowding neither row.

### Window
- Freely resizable; clock scales proportionally to fill the window.
- Sensible minimum size; window frame persists across launches.
- Clean look: hidden/minimal title bar, dark clock-card styling.

### Always-on-top
- `⌘1` toggles the window between normal level and floating (above all windows).
- The window does **not** follow the user across Spaces: it stays on the desktop where it lives, and floating applies only on that Space. Swiping to another desktop leaves the clock behind.
- Must work with both the main-row `1` and the numeric-keypad `1`.
- In-app shortcut is sufficient for v1 (app focused). Subtle visual indicator of the pinned state (e.g. small pin glyph).
- Also toggleable from the app menu.

### Time announcements
- Announces the current time, e.g. "10:07 PM", exactly when the minute ticks over.
- Cadence setting: Off (default) / Every hour (on the hour) / Every 15 minutes / Custom every N minutes (N = 1–180, announced when minute % N == 0, anchored to the hour).
- **Two convey methods, independently switchable.** The cadence decides *when* a boundary happens; the "Convey with" toggles decide *how* it is delivered — `speakOnCadence` (default on, which is what the cadence has always meant) speaks it aloud, and `notifyOnCadence` (default off) posts it as a macOS user notification. Both may be on, in which case the boundary speaks and posts. Both may also be off: the cadence fill still counts down to the boundary and nothing fires when it arrives. That is a deliberate configuration, not a bug — the fill is a visual countdown in its own right — but it is the one combination that makes the cadence otherwise invisible. Neither toggle affects the fill or `CadenceSchedule`, which are tied to the cadence itself.
- Notification defaults off because switching it *on* is what triggers the system permission prompt. Asking at launch, before the user has expressed any interest, earns a reflexive "Don't Allow", and that answer is sticky — only System Settings can reverse it. Same rule the events panel follows for calendar access.
- Settings persist via UserDefaults (@AppStorage). Accessible from a small settings UI (menu or gear popover).
- **Cadence countdown fill**: while a cadence is set, a rounded panel sits behind the flip cards — a dark warm amber that reads as a lit ground against the black, well below the digits and cards in luminance so nothing on top of it loses contrast, and far enough from red not to be confused with the events timeline's now-line. It fills the face region completely the moment the clock speaks, then drains **right to left** linearly with the time remaining: the black ground is uncovered from the right and the surviving colour hugs the left edge, its width being the fraction of the wait still ahead (half the interval gone → left half still lit), vanishing off the left at the boundary. The refill sweeps back to full with a ~1.5 s ease-out so it swells rather than flashes. A `TimelineView` ticking once a second drives the wipe, since the face itself only changes once a minute. The panel keeps its full-size rounded silhouette and is revealed through a leading-anchored mask, so the corners stay soft while the moving edge is a clean vertical cut — shrinking the shape itself would round the leading edge and read as a pill sliding away rather than a wipe. It lives inside the face's own region, so it never spills onto the events panel or the divider, and its corner radius scales with the face unit.
- Boundaries come from one pure value type, `CadenceSchedule`, which the announcement, the notification and the countdown all read, so they can never disagree. Boundaries are anchored to the top of the hour in every mode: a minute-of-hour is a boundary when it is a multiple of the step. That makes the top of the hour a boundary always, which matters when the step doesn't divide 60 — with N = 7 the boundaries are :00, :07 … :56 and then :00 of the next hour, so the final gap of the hour is 4 minutes, not 7. The countdown divides by the actual gap between the surrounding boundaries rather than by a nominal N, so the panel always starts full and always reaches empty.
- The fill is hidden with cadence Off (there is nothing to count down to) and can be switched off outright via the "Show cadence fill" setting (`showCadenceFill`, default on). The fraction is recomputed from the current settings on every tick, so changing the cadence retargets the countdown immediately. The spacebar's on-demand speech is deliberately outside the schedule and leaves the countdown untouched.
- On-demand: with the app focused, the **spacebar** speaks the current time immediately, independent of the cadence setting (it works even when cadence is Off). Presses carrying ⌘/⌥/⌃ pass through so menu and system shortcuts aren't shadowed, as do presses while a text field is being edited. Rapid taps restart the utterance rather than queueing. Also available as the "Speak Time" menu command.

#### Wording
Both convey methods word the time through one helper, `TimePhrase`, so a boundary can never speak 10:07 and post 10:08. It offers two forms: `display` (`10:07 PM`, the notification title) and `spoken` (`It's 10:07 PM`, or `It's 10 o'clock` on the hour, because some voices garble "10:00 PM"). The formatters are pinned to `en_US_POSIX` with an explicit 12-hour pattern rather than a locale style — the spoken phrase is hand-written English, and a 24-hour locale would read "It's 22:07" aloud. `TimePhrase` is main-actor isolated because `DateFormatter` is a non-`Sendable` reference type and every caller already runs there.

#### Notifications (UserNotifications)
- `NotificationAnnouncer` wraps `UNUserNotificationCenter`: `requestAuthorizationIfNeeded()` (only while the answer is still `notDetermined` — once decided, the framework merely replays the decision) and `notify(_:)`, which posts immediately (`trigger: nil`). Authorization is tracked as a three-state value — not-determined / authorized / denied, with `provisional` and `ephemeral` folded into authorized — and re-read every time the Settings window appears, since the user can revoke permission in System Settings while the app runs and nothing tells us when they do.
- **Works from the ad-hoc-signed bundle.** Verified on macOS 26: `requestAuthorization` shows the real system prompt, and notifications post and appear, with no developer certificate and no notarization. It does require a *bundle* — `UNUserNotificationCenter.current()` traps in a process with no bundle identifier, so this is reachable only from `dist/Flapjack.app`, never from a bare `swift run`.
- **The app is its own `UNUserNotificationCenterDelegate`**, returning `[.banner, .list, .sound]` from `willPresent`. This is not optional: macOS suppresses banners for the frontmost app, and a desktop clock is very often exactly that. Verified — without the delegate the notification arrived and filed itself silently into Notification Center with nothing ever appearing on screen.
- **Each new time check withdraws the previous one**, so a 15-minute cadence doesn't leave 96 stale entries a day piled up. The obvious implementation — one fixed request identifier — is wrong, and was measured to be: the system treats a repeat under the same identifier as an *edit* of the already-delivered notification and updates it in place, silently, with no banner. So each post takes a fresh UUID identifier (which the system alerts for) and the delivered ones are withdrawn first, awaited before the add so the new notification can't be caught by its own sweep. Withdrawing everything delivered rather than a remembered identifier is deliberate: the time check is the only notification Flapjack posts, so anything sitting there is stale — including leftovers from a previous run.
- **Persistence is the user's setting, not the app's.** Whether a notification lingers until dismissed or fades after a few seconds is the per-app **alert style** in System Settings → Notifications → Flapjack; an app cannot force it. macOS 26 names the two "Persistent" and "Temporary" (older releases: "Alerts" and "Banners"), so the in-product caption under the Notification toggle branches on the OS version, the same way the voice-download hint does.
- Settings deep-links to the app's own row with `x-apple.systempreferences:com.apple.Notifications-Settings.extension?id=com.s4lly.flapjack` — verified on macOS 26 to land directly on the Flapjack pane rather than the Notifications root; the bare extension URL follows as a fallback. The hint and button appear only when authorization is denied, since that is the only state the user has to leave the app to fix.
- The spacebar's on-demand speech stays voice-only and ignores both toggles: it is a "what time is it?" trigger, not an announcement.

#### Voice
`AVSpeechSynthesizer` is the only speech synthesis API available to a third-party Mac app: Siri's voices are reserved for system services and cannot be reached through `AVSpeechSynthesisVoice` (verified on macOS 26), `NSSpeechSynthesizer` is deprecated as of macOS 14 and exposes the same voice set, macOS 26 added no new synthesis API (its new `SpeechAnalyzer`/`SpeechTranscriber` are speech-to-*text*), and Personal Voice needs a voice the user has recorded plus a TCC authorization an ad-hoc-signed app can't obtain. So the quality of the announcement is decided entirely by which voices are *installed*, and every Mac ships with only compact ones — which is what "robotic" means.

- **`VoiceCatalog` owns the choice.** It reads `AVSpeechSynthesisVoice.speechVoices()` and offers: anything above `.default` quality (its presence proves the user downloaded it deliberately, whatever the language), plus `.default` voices in the user's leading preferred languages. English is always kept, so the clock always has something to speak with.
- **Excluded outright**: voices the system flags with the `isNoveltyVoice` trait, everything under `com.apple.speech.synthesis.voice.` (the MacinTalk set — Zarvox and friends, but also Fred/Junior/Kathy/Ralph, which carry no novelty trait and sound just as wrong), and everything under `com.apple.eloquence.` (DECtalk-lineage, robotic by design). A stock Mac carries ~130 of these; unfiltered they would bury the one or two usable entries.
- Only the first `preferredLanguageDepth` (3) entries of `Locale.preferredLanguages` count. macOS seeds that list with every localisation it ships (~35 entries), so honouring all of it would offer a voice in every language on earth.
- **Ordering**: quality (premium → enhanced → default), then how high the language ranks for the user, then `compact` ahead of `super-compact`, then name. The first entry is by definition the best available voice, which is exactly what the "Automatic" setting means.
- The setting (`voiceIdentifier`, default `""` = automatic) stores the voice *identifier*, not an index, so installing or removing voices can never silently repoint the choice. It is resolved per utterance rather than cached: an identifier that is no longer installed falls back to automatic, and a voice downloaded while the app runs takes effect at the next announcement.
- Settings shows the picker with a **Test** button that speaks the current time immediately, so a voice can be auditioned without waiting for a cadence boundary. The list is rebuilt when the window appears and on `AVSpeechSynthesizer.availableVoicesDidChangeNotification`, which fires when a download completes.
- When the best installed voice is still `.default`, Settings shows a hint plus an **Open System Settings…** button deep-linking to `x-apple.systempreferences:com.apple.preference.universalaccess?SpokenContent`. The pane became an extension bundle in Ventura but still declares the old prefPane id as its `legacyBundleIdentifier`, so that URL works from Ventura through macOS 26; the `com.apple.Accessibility-Settings.extension` form follows as a hedge. The `SpokenContent` anchor is what makes it land on the pane instead of the Accessibility root. macOS 26 renamed the pane "Read & Speak", so the copy branches on the OS version.

#### Ducking other apps' audio
Speaking the time over a loud Spotify track is inaudible, so `duckOtherAudio` (default off) turns running music players down for the length of an utterance and puts them straight back.

**macOS gives a third-party app no way to do this properly.** All three obvious routes were checked against the macOS 26 SDK and all three are closed:
- **`AVAudioSession` ducking** (`AVAudioSessionCategoryOptionDuckOthers`) is the iOS answer. `AVAudioSession` is declared `API_UNAVAILABLE(macos)` — the class and every member; it appears in the macOS SDK headers only for Catalyst.
- **Per-app volume** has no public API. Core Audio's HAL exposes volume on *devices*; the process objects it gained in macOS 14 carry only `PID`, `BundleID`, `Devices` and `IsRunning`/`IsRunningInput`/`IsRunningOutput` — no volume property of any kind. Tools like SoundSource do it by installing an audio driver, which is a different category of product.
- **Routing our own speech somewhere else**, so the *system* output volume could be ducked instead, is likewise unavailable: `AVSpeechUtterance.outputChannels` and `AVSpeechSynthesizer.usesApplicationAudioSession` are both `API_UNAVAILABLE(macos)` (as is `mixToTelephonyUplink`). Speech leaves by the default output device like everything else, so turning that device down would mute the announcement along with the music.

What is available is that the loud apps are usually **scriptable**. Spotify and Music each publish a read/write integer `sound volume` (0–100) and a read-only `player state`, so `AudioDucker` asks each one for its own volume and hands it back afterwards. **The limitation is exactly that, and it is stated in the UI**: a player with no scripting dictionary — a browser tab, a game, a video call — cannot be ducked, and no fallback would work.

- **The table is the feature.** `AudioDucker.players` maps a bundle identifier (for "is it running?") to an AppleScript application name (for the `tell` line). Adding another scriptable player is one line; nothing else is player-specific. Nothing user-supplied ever reaches the script source.
- **Never launches anything.** `tell application "X"` *starts* X if it isn't running, which would be an absurd side effect of speaking the time, so every send is gated on `NSRunningApplication` — on the way down and on the way back up. Verified: Music stayed unlaunched across a full run of duck tests.
- **Only ducks what is actually playing.** A paused player isn't drowning anything out, and moving its volume would just be meddling. Volume and playing-ness come back in one round trip, tested against the `playing` enumerator (which both apps share) rather than coerced to text (which they spell differently).
- **Ducks, doesn't cut**: four steps to a quarter of the original over ~0.3 s, and the same ramp back. A hard jump reads as a glitch.
- **Restores exactly, and this needs a correction step.** Spotify quantises the volume it is given and reads back a point *lower* than the number that was set — measured: `set 69` reads 68 while `set 70` reads 69. Handing the saved number back literally would therefore walk the user's volume down by one on *every* announcement, fading their music out over a day on an hourly cadence. So a restore ends by re-reading and, if the player didn't land on the saved value, correcting by the observed error, twice at most. The read is taken after a ~0.25 s pause, which is load-bearing: asked immediately after a set, Spotify still answers with the *previous* ramp step, making the error look like 13 rather than 1 — over the sanity bound and so declined. Errors larger than two points are deliberately left alone; that is not rounding, that is the user reaching for their own volume control mid-utterance, and their number wins. Measured: three consecutive announcements start and end at exactly 69.
- **One duck per burst of speech.** `Announcer` ducks before `speak` and restores from `AVSpeechSynthesizerDelegate`'s `didFinish`/`didCancel` — but `speak` cancels whatever is in flight, and that cancellation arrives as a callback for the *outgoing* utterance, which must not be read as "the speech is over". So the announcer records which utterance is current (before calling `stopSpeaking`, since the cancel can be delivered synchronously) and only that one's ending lifts the duck. `duck()` is itself idempotent while ducked, so the *original* volumes survive a re-trigger. Verified: two spacebars 0.5 s apart produce two utterances but exactly one duck and one restore, ending on the original volume.
- **Safety nets.** A 15 s watchdog force-restores if the delegate callbacks never arrive, and `NSApplicationWillTerminate` restores synchronously — a quit mid-utterance would otherwise strand the user's music at a quarter volume with nothing left running to fix it. Because all script work is on one serial queue, a restore enqueued while the duck is still ramping simply runs after it, and still sees the saved volumes.
- **Threading**: `NSAppleScript` is blocking and not thread-safe, so all of it runs on one private serial queue (in-process rather than shelling out to `osascript`: no process spawn per step, and the error number comes back structured). The queue's ordering is what makes "restore always sees what duck saved" true without a lock. Observable state stays on the main actor.
- **Permission.** The first Apple Event to each player raises the system's Automation prompt, which is why the setting defaults off — the same rule notifications and the calendar follow. `NSAppleEventsUsageDescription` in `Info.plist` supplies the reason shown in that prompt, and consent is per *target*, so Spotify and Music are asked for separately. Verified working from the ad-hoc-signed bundle on macOS 26, with consent keyed to the stable `com.s4lly.flapjack` identifier so it survives rebuilds. A refusal (`errAEEventNotPermitted`, -1743) is surfaced to Settings, which then shows a hint and a button deep-linking to `x-apple.systempreferences:com.apple.preference.security?Privacy_Automation` — verified on macOS 26 to land directly on the Automation pane. A player that quits mid-utterance returns `procNotFound` (-600) and is skipped rather than treated as an error.
- The toggle sits with the convey methods but is deliberately **not** disabled with cadence Off: the spacebar speaks regardless of the cadence, and it is the trigger most likely to land in the middle of a loud track.

### Events panel (EventKit)
- Shows today's remaining calendar events beside the clock. Strictly opt-in: placement is a three-state setting — Off (default) / Column (beside the face) / Below (under the face) — cycled Off → Column → Below → Off.
- Data comes from the macOS calendar store via EventKit, so any account the user has synced there is included (iCloud, Exchange, and Google via System Settings → Internet Accounts). Flapjack talks to no calendar service directly and does no networking.
- Access is requested when the user first turns the panel on (Off → Column), or at launch if the panel was left visible in a previous session — never for a panel that is Off. The three app-level states are not-determined / granted / denied; write-only and restricted count as denied, since neither can read events. Denied is surfaced to the UI so it can explain how to grant access in System Settings, rather than showing an empty list.
- The fetch window runs from "now" to the end of the current day, across all calendars. All-day events sort first, then everything else by start time.
- Refresh triggers: the existing minute tick (so past events drop off as the clock advances), `EKEventStoreChanged` notifications (edits made in Calendar or an account sync), and system wake — the clock engine exposes a resync hook separate from the minute tick, because a wake is not a minute boundary and must not trigger a spoken announcement. Refresh is skipped entirely while placement is Off or access isn't granted.
- Events reach the views as a plain `EventItem` value type (title, start/end, all-day flag, calendar name and colour). No EventKit type crosses into the UI, so a future backend-brokered event source can fill the same shape.
- `⌘2` (main row or numeric keypad) cycles the placement, and the View menu carries the same command with its current state in the title — "Events: Off" / "Events: Right Column" / "Events: Below" — since a cycling command can't show a checkmark honestly.
- A row is one line: a small per-calendar colour dot, the start time (locale short form with the AM/PM designator dropped — the panel only covers the rest of today — or "all-day"), then the title, truncated at the tail. Dim greys on black, matching the face: time in a tabular/monospaced grey, title slightly brighter. All type is scaled from a single unit derived from the panel's own size, so the panel stays legible whether the window is tiny or full-screen.
- Non-list states use the same dim hint style: empty is "No more events today", not-determined shows "Connecting…" (the access request is in flight, triggered by the cycle), and denied spells out "Calendar access needed — grant in System Settings → Privacy & Security → Calendars" in smaller, wrapping text.
- Column layout: a vertical day timeline on the trailing edge, scrollable vertically with hidden scroll indicators. Below layout: a horizontal day timeline under the face, scrollable horizontally.
- The split is the user's, not a fixed number: a **divider bar** sits on the seam — vertical in Column (dragged left/right), horizontal in Below (dragged up/down) — and drags the panel's share live, the face and the timeline both reflowing during the drag rather than on release. Two fractions persist independently, `eventsColumnFraction` (default 0.34) and `eventsBelowFraction` (default 0.30), written through `AppSettings` on every drag step so an unexpected quit keeps the split.
- The fraction is bounded to 0.15–0.5 — the face always keeps the larger half — and then clamped in points so both halves stay usable at the minimum window size: the panel floors at 120 pt (column) / 64 pt (strip) and the face keeps at least 150 pt wide / 50 pt tall, minus the 9 pt divider gutter. Because the stored value is a fraction, resizing the window reapplies the split proportionally.
- The divider reads as a hairline (white at 10% opacity) with a three-dot grip at its centre; the whole 9 pt gutter is the hit area, hovering or dragging lifts the line and dots to a brighter grey, and the pointer becomes the appropriate resize cursor over it. Its mouse handling is a real `NSView` (`SplitDivider`'s handle) answering `mouseDownCanMoveWindow = false` — the window is movable by its background, and AppKit decides that before SwiftUI sees the click, so a plain `DragGesture` would lose every drag to a window move.
- The panel's space is reserved before the face is measured: the face's unit is computed from the window minus the column's width (or the strip's height) and the divider gutter, keeping the existing ~3% margin, so the digits rescale — and, per the adaptive rule above, may switch between across and stacked — as the divider moves. With placement Off the face gets the whole window exactly as it did before the panel existed, and in every mode the flip animation and the in-card AM/PM badge are untouched — the panel is a sibling of the face, never an overlay.

### Architecture notes
- SwiftPM executable target, packaged into `dist/Flapjack.app` by `scripts/build-app.sh` (no Xcode required, ad-hoc codesigned if needed).
- Keep state in a small observable model (`ClockModel`) ticking on minute boundaries; announcements driven by the same tick.
- Design for a future sync layer: settings/state mutations go through the model, not scattered view code, so a cloud-sync backend can be attached later. No networking in v1.
